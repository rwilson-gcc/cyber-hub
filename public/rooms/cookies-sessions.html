Cookies & sessions: the trust you carry in your pocket

HTTP forgets you the moment a page loads. Cookies are how a site remembers who you are between clicks — and how an attacker can borrow your identity if they're handled carelessly.

Introduction

HTTP is stateless: every request is handled as if it's the first time you've ever visited. So how does a site remember you're logged in as you move from page to page? It asks your browser to carry a small piece of data, a cookie, and show it on every later request.

Why does it matter?

A session cookie is often the only thing standing between "just anyone" and "logged in as you." If that cookie can be read by a malicious script, guessed because it's predictable, or sent somewhere it shouldn't be, an attacker can step into your session without ever knowing your password.

Where does this happen in real life?

Session hijacking and cookie theft are behind real account takeovers every year, often through missing cookie protections rather than some exotic exploit. Fixing it is usually a one-line configuration change, which is exactly why it's worth learning to spot.

An analogy

A session cookie is like a festival wristband. Once staff see it on your wrist, they assume it's you and wave you through. They don't check your face every time. If someone can copy your wristband, or if the wristbands are just numbered 1001, 1002, 1003 in order, that trust breaks down fast.

Stay legal

Theory

Idea 1: cookies carry state across a stateless protocol

  1. 1. You log inServer checks your password, then issues a session cookie.
  2. 2. Browser stores itThe cookie sits in your browser, tied to that site.
  3. 3. Sent every timeEach later request to that site includes the cookie automatically.

Idea 2: cookie attributes are the small print that matters

AttributeWhat it doesRisk if missing
HttpOnlyStops JavaScript on the page from reading the cookieA script injected via XSS could steal the session cookie directly
SecureOnly sends the cookie over HTTPSCookie could be intercepted on an insecure connection
SameSiteControls whether the cookie is sent on requests from other sitesCan enable cross-site request forgery (CSRF)
Expires / Max-AgeSets when the cookie stops being validA cookie that never expires stays dangerous indefinitely if stolen

Idea 3: predictable session IDs

A session ID should look random and be hard to guess, something like 9f3a7c21e0.... If instead sessions count up one at a time, like 1001, 1002, 1003, an attacker who gets one valid session can simply try nearby numbers to land inside someone else's.

Idea 4: don't store secrets readably in a cookie

A cookie is just text sitting on the user's machine. Anyone with access to that browser, or anyone who intercepts the cookie, can read it. Encoding it with Base64 hides it from a casual glance, but not from anyone who knows to decode it — exactly like the Base64 you decoded in the recon room.

Demonstration

Worked example on a fictional forum, oldtoon-forum.example:

Set-Cookie: session=4021
Set-Cookie: theme=dark; Secure; SameSite=Lax

The session cookie has no HttpOnly, no Secure, and its value is just a small incrementing number. Put together, that's three separate weaknesses in one line: a script could read it, it could travel over plain HTTP, and it's easy to guess a neighbouring value. Any one of these is worth reporting; all three together make it urgent.

The fix is small: add HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite=Strict and generate session IDs from a long random value instead of a counter.

Key takeaways

  • Cookies let a stateless protocol remember who you are between requests.
  • HttpOnly, Secure and SameSite each defend against a different kind of attack.
  • A predictable session ID can be guessed, no stealing required.
  • Encoding a cookie value isn't the same as protecting it.
  • Small, free configuration changes close most cookie weaknesses.
