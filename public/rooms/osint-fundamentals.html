OSINT fundamentals: following the digital footprint

Open-source intelligence, OSINT, means building a picture of someone or something using only information that is already public. No hacking, no passwords: just careful reading, cross-referencing, and knowing where people tend to overshare.

Introduction

OSINT (open-source intelligence) is the practice of gathering information from sources anyone can access: social profiles, forum posts, company websites, archived pages, public records. It needs no special access and breaks no law on its own, which is exactly why it is often the first step of both a legitimate security assessment and a real attack.

Why does it matter?

Attackers use OSINT to build a target profile before a phishing attempt or a social-engineering call: a pet’s name for a security question, a job title for a convincing pretext, a manager’s name to impersonate in an urgent email. Defenders use the same skills the other way round: to see what a determined attacker could already find out about their organisation, and to help staff understand what they are quietly giving away.

Where does this happen in real life?

Authorised penetration tests and red team engagements usually start with an OSINT phase. Real incidents regularly trace back to information an attacker found simply by reading what was already public.

An analogy

A good OSINT investigator works like a librarian, not a burglar: every source they use was already open to the public. The skill is knowing which shelves to check, and noticing when the same name shows up in more than one place.

Stay legal and ethical

Using these techniques against a real person without a legitimate, authorised reason (such as a permitted assessment) can still cause real harm and may break stalking, harassment or data protection law, even though every individual source is public. Learning objectives

  • Explain what makes information "open source" and why that doesn’t make it safe to use however you like.
  • Correlate a username across more than one platform to build a fuller picture.
  • Use an archived or cached version of a page to find information since removed.
  • Combine several small, individually harmless facts into one finding.
  • Describe what someone could do to reduce their own public footprint.

Theory

Idea 1: small facts add up

No single public post usually gives away much on its own. A job title here, a gym’s name there, a pet’s name used as a throwaway joke. OSINT is rarely about finding one big leak: it is about collecting several small, harmless-looking facts and noticing what they add up to.

  1. 1. Find a handleA username, email pattern, or display name to search on.
  2. 2. Cross-referenceSearch that same handle across other platforms.
  3. 3. Check historyArchived or cached pages can hold what was since deleted.
  4. 4. CombinePut the small facts together into one picture.

Idea 2: the same username is a thread to pull

People often reuse a distinctive username across several unrelated sites, sometimes for convenience and sometimes without thinking about it. Finding that same handle on a second platform, especially one with a looser privacy setting, can reveal a real name, employer, or location that the first platform kept hidden.

Mini quiz: why reuse matters

Someone uses the handle "harbour_jross82" on a professional forum (no name shown) and also on a public fitness app (full name shown). What does that let an investigator do?

Idea 3: the internet rarely forgets, even after deletion

Archiving services periodically save snapshots of public web pages. A company page edited or taken down today may still exist in an earlier snapshot, complete with an old staff list, a phone number, or a line since quietly removed. Treat anything ever made public as potentially permanent.

Mini quiz: archived pages

A company removes a staff directory page from its live website after a privacy review. Is that information necessarily gone?

Idea 4: reducing your own footprint

  • Use different, unrelated usernames across unrelated platforms where privacy matters.
  • Check what old, forgotten accounts and posts are still public, not just current ones.
  • Avoid combining a real name with identifying personal details (routine, location, family) in the same public post.
  • Remember that deleting a post does not guarantee it was never archived.

Demonstration

Worked example. A tester, authorised to assess a fictional firm glenmuir-partners.example, starts with a job listing that names a contact: "Questions? Ask R. Thorn in IT."

Step 1: search "R. Thorn glenmuir-partners" → a forum post signed "rthorn_glenmuir", asking for help with a home network issue, including the make of router.
Step 2: search "rthorn_glenmuir" on other platforms → the same handle on a public fitness app, full name "Robbie Thorn", home suburb visible from a running route.
Step 3: check an archived snapshot of the company’s old "About us" page → Robbie Thorn listed as "IT Support, extension 214", since removed from the live site.

None of these three facts alone is dangerous. Together, a tester now has a real name, a job role, a direct extension, a home area, and a plausible pretext ("hi, it’s IT, following up about your home network") for a phishing or vishing attempt, built entirely from information Robbie never thought to connect.

Reflection questions

Discussion points

The shared username. Without WoodburnJR appearing on more than one platform, the forum post and the hiking app would never have been connected to the same person.

Discussion points

It gives a specific, current job responsibility (administering the new stock system) that a phisher could build a highly relevant, believable pretext around, rather than just a name or a location.

Discussion points

Individually each fact is mundane: a hobby, a hiking route, an old staff listing. Combined, they give a name, a role, a direct line, a routine, and a live project to impersonate convincingly, which is a different level of risk entirely.

Discussion points

Examples: avoid using the same distinctive username across professional and personal platforms, especially where one shows a full name; be cautious about posting a recognisable daily routine (same route, same time) publicly; check what old company pages or directories might still be archived and ask for genuinely outdated listings to be requested for removal where possible.

Discussion points

Even lawfully public information, when deliberately gathered and combined about a specific person without a legitimate reason, can enable stalking, harassment, or targeted fraud, and may breach data protection law depending on how it is collected, stored or used. Legality of the source does not excuse a harmful purpose.

Key takeaways

  • OSINT uses only publicly available information, which is exactly why it is so effective.
  • A reused username is one of the easiest threads to pull between otherwise separate profiles.
  • Archived or cached pages can outlive what a live site currently shows.
  • Small, individually harmless facts can combine into a genuinely risky profile.
  • Using these same techniques against a real person without a legitimate reason can still cause real harm.
